You are reading a short window of real messages from a Burning Man theme
camp's WhatsApp group chat, already stripped of anything personal. The
messages are casual, fragmentary, half-finished, full of reactions and inside
references — exactly how a group chat actually sounds. That is the point:
this camp's idiom, its dry humor, its directness, its Burning Man register,
lives in these messages and nowhere else. Nothing else in this pipeline
teaches that.

The output does not sound like this. Do not write a chat message. Do not
address a specific reply, do not use chat abbreviations, reaction words
("lol", "same", "this ☝️"), emoji, or a half-finished thought the way a text
leaves one. Write answer-shaped prose instead: two to five full sentences
that read like a camp member sitting down and explaining the topic in the
conversation to someone who wasn't there — a standalone explanation, not a
reply and not a transcript.

For each pair:

- **instruction** — a natural question someone might ask about the subject
  this window is about. A question about the topic, not about the chat
  itself ("what did they say").
- **response** — the answer-shaped explanation described above, written in
  this camp's voice: its rhythm, its dryness, the way it actually talks. Use
  only what the conversation supports. Do not add a name, date, number, or
  detail the messages do not establish, and do not name a specific camp
  member — refer to a role or "someone on the team" instead. This pass
  teaches how the camp sounds, not who did what; a name in the answer is a
  fact leaking into a voice lesson.
- **supporting_message_ids** — the ids (the bracketed number before each
  line) this pair actually draws from. At least one, and only ones you
  actually used.

A window that is too thin, too fragmented, or too much reaction-only
chatter to support a real explanation returns an empty list. That is a
correct answer — do not stretch three emoji and a "lol" into an essay.

Never lift a sentence from the messages verbatim. Paraphrase throughout; the
point is voice, not quotation.
