Case study · iOS · on-device LLM · 2026

Lucy
Offline AI

An iPhone app that answers questions about one Burning Man camp out of ten years of its own group chat, with a three-billion-parameter language model running on the phone and no network at all. This page explains the problem, the architecture that came out of it, and the places it broke on the way there.

The problem

Preservation Society is a theme camp at Burning Man: a few hundred people, a decade of accumulated know-how, and one week a year when all of it matters at once. Which socket the lag bolts take. Where the water barrels went last year. Who has the key to the generator. Every one of those answers exists. They are just buried in 27,000 WhatsApp messages and a folder of spreadsheets, and the person who knows is asleep, or on the other side of the city, or not coming this year.

The obvious answer is a chatbot over the chat history. Three things make that hard in this particular place.

  • There is no network. Black Rock City has no usable signal. Whatever the app can answer, it has to be carrying already: the knowledge, the search index, and the language model itself, on a phone that is also somebody's camera and torch.
  • The knowledge is conversation, not facts. Group chat is people talking. A fact like "the padlock hammer lives inside the van" is spread across three messages, two years apart, one of which is a joke. Something has to read all of it and decide what the camp actually knows.
  • A confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. Nobody can check. If the app says the ladders are in the wrong truck, somebody walks to the wrong truck at 2am in a dust storm. A language model left to itself will say that kind of thing fluently and often.

Why the model is not allowed to know things

A language model is, at heart, a very good guesser of the next word. It has read most of the internet. It has read none of this camp's chat. Ask it where the camp keeps its shade structure and it will produce a perfectly plausible sentence, because producing plausible sentences is the one thing it does. It has no way to tell the difference between what it knows and what merely sounds right.

So Lucy is built on one rule, and everything else on this page is in service of it:

Retrieval supplies the facts. The model only phrases them. Anything particular to the camp, a name, a date, a number, a place, who did what, has to come from a row in a database. The model's job is to turn those rows into a sentence a person would say. A claim the model made up is a bug, not a style problem.

This is the same idea usually called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG: look the facts up first, then hand them to the model with the question. What is unusual here is how far it is pushed, and that it is measured. On forty of the camp's real questions, the model with no grounding rules invented a camp-specific claim in 82% of answers. With the rules, 2%. That number, not anyone's taste, decides what ships.

The rule has one deliberate exception. What the model knows about the world in general stays its own. She is allowed to know the desert is hot and that you should drink before you are thirsty. She is not allowed to guess which of the camp's two vans the water is in. The line is ownership: general knowledge is hers; the camp's particulars need a row.

The shape of it

Two halves, with a database between them. Think of a librarian who does all the reading and cataloguing before the trip, and then on the trip only ever looks things up and reads them aloud. The expensive thinking happens once, on a Mac with an internet connection, weeks before the event. The phone never has to understand the chat history. It only has to search a catalogue and phrase what it finds.

Build time: turning chat into knowledge

The pipeline is twelve stages of Python. Most of the interesting decisions are in three of them.

1. Reading the chat

A WhatsApp export is a text file of timestamped lines, full of system notices, attachment markers and invisible direction characters. The parser turns ten group chats into 27,163 messages, each with a sender, a time and a body. The camp's documents, spreadsheets, manuals, menus, are converted to Markdown and CSV and read alongside.

Most of the documents were noise. 627 became 144, a 77% cut, and the question was how to cut without losing something that mattered. Three reasonable rules were tried and each deleted something load-bearing: dropping the finance folder removed the only document containing the word "tourniquet", because the medical kit inventory was filed under finance. The rule that survived is a test rather than a rule: a document may be dropped only if every distinctive word in it appears somewhere in the kept set. If a word would be orphaned, the document stays.

2. Enrichment: deciding what the camp knows

This is the step that makes the whole thing work, and it is judgement, not parsing. A large model, Gemini running in Google's cloud, reads the messages and decides that a phrase is a thing the camp has a name for, that a rambling thread contains a decision, that a running joke is actually a tradition. It writes what it finds as claims: short dated sentences in the camp's own voice.

Three messages about a wrench and a socket size become one claim, with a year, filed under the shade structure. The claim is stored next to an evidence row pointing at the exact messages it came from. There are 8,020 of those evidence rows behind 1,745 camp facts, and a database trigger refuses any claim that arrives without one. When she answers, she can say which message she is leaning on, and the app can show it.

The choice of model here was measured, not assumed. On a byte-identical corpus, Gemini's strongest model found 149 distinct entities where its fast model found 64. The hundred it missed were not inventory. They were the camp's culture: the named traditions, the in-jokes, the things you cannot photograph. Capability beats throughput when the job is judgement.

Enrichment costs money and takes hours, which used to mean every experiment took a whole run. Every request is now cached by a hash of the model, the prompt and the message content, never by row number, because a rebuild renumbers every row and a cache keyed by id would return the right answer for the wrong message and look healthy doing it. A rebuild pays only for what changed. Six per cent of the messages were new; the other ninety-four came from the cache.

3. Embeddings: searching by meaning

Word search has a blind spot: "med kit" does not match "first aid", and "where do we dump the grey water" shares no words with "the evap pond is behind the kitchen". An embedding fixes part of that. A small model turns a sentence into a list of 768 numbers, a point in a space where sentences that mean similar things end up near each other. Every claim gets its vector at build time. On the phone, the question gets a vector too, and the nearest claims come back even when they share no words.

The encoder is EmbeddingGemma, 300 million parameters, the same model on the Mac and on the phone. That matters: vectors from two different models are not comparable, and a mismatch does not fail, it just returns nonsense similarities. The packaging gate checks that every claim has a vector from the model the phone will use.

Run time: answering a question on the phone

Take a real shape of question: "how do we get water delivered?" Here is what happens between the tap and the answer, all of it on the phone.

1. Words

The question is lowercased and split, and words that carry no signal are dropped: how do we get goes, water delivered stays. The stop list is explicit, not a length rule. An earlier version dropped every word of two letters or fewer, which silently deleted a camper called Oz from every question about him. Six of 71 real questions lost every term that way.

2. Word search, five tables at once

The terms are matched against the camp's facts, its named things (vehicles, traditions, places), its people, its documents, and its stories. Each hit is scored. An exact topic match is worth eight points; a word in the text less. Then age multiplies the score: 0.88 per year, floored at 0.35, so a 2019 fact still competes but a 2025 one wins a tie. Undated facts get 0.6, roughly a 2021 claim, because the honest prior for an unknown year is a typical year. An earlier version scored undated facts at 1.0, which handed every claim with missing metadata a threefold advantage over anything dated. A test caught it: a question about water delivery started preferring an undated row about vouchers over the only row in the table containing the word "deliver".

3. Meaning search, capped hard

The question is encoded into its 768 numbers by the same EmbeddingGemma model, running on the CPU, and compared against every stored vector. Only claims scoring above 0.45 count, and at most two are added. Both numbers were learned the hard way, in the section below.

4. The fact sheet

Everything that matched is rendered into plain text with its date and where it came from. This is the entire world the model is allowed to see:

FACTS YOU MAY USE:
- Water comes by truck on Tuesday and Friday;
  book it on the shared sheet (2024) [from our manual]
- Grey water goes to the evap pond behind
  the kitchen (2023)
About Doris, our vehicle:
- carries the water barrels to playa [said 2022-08-19]

QUESTION: how do we get water delivered?

Illustrative rows, not the camp's.

If nothing matched, the sheet says so, in words, and the rules tell her to say she does not know rather than fill the gap. If the question named a person, a short portrait of them leads the sheet: what they are like, how they show up in chat, then what they do. That order was reversed once and she answered "who is X" by reciting a job description.

5. The prompt, and two dials

The sheet goes to the model wrapped in a prompt of five numbered rules, the first of which is the one above. The rest: answer the question that was asked and name the gap if the detail is missing; credit the camp, not yourself, for building and hauling things; when facts disagree, give both with dates; if asked to mock a campmate, decline warmly. Two paragraphs are user settings: register, from dry to loose, and length, from terse to full. They move her tone without touching the grounding.

One rule about the prompt itself: it contains no example sentences. An earlier version illustrated its rules with sample answers, and she pasted them back as answers. It got worse after a parsing bug was fixed, because a model that reads the prompt properly copies its examples faithfully.

6. The model

Gemma 4 E2B, quantised to four bits (3.11 GB), through llama.cpp compiled into the app. Phones with less memory get Gemma 3 1B (806 MB) instead, chosen by RAM at first launch, with a manual override because an 8 GB iPhone 16 Pro reports 7.46 GiB usable and fell on the wrong side of the threshold. A memory spike on a real device settled that a 3 GB model would fit at all: with Apple's increased-memory entitlement the app sees 6.1 GB, and memory-mapping the weights means loading costs 720 MB of resident memory rather than 3.6 GB.

The two Gemma generations use different chat markers, the tokens that say "the user's turn ends here". The app decides which to use by tokenising a probe string and counting tokens, never by filename. For the first week the markers were hardcoded to the older model's, so every prompt reached the larger model wrapped in text it read as ordinary characters. Nothing looked broken. It answered fluently. Every prompt tuned in that week was tuned against malformed input.

7. Memory that survives a reboot

There is no rolling transcript in the prompt. Every exchange is written to a second SQLite database, separate from the camp's knowledge so it survives app updates, and comes back the same way a fact does: the last three turns always, plus any older turn that matches the question. History is labelled as conversation, never as fact, because she can be told something wrong and must not repeat it back as the camp's record. A follow-up like "where is it stored" works because the previous question's terms blend into this one, but only when the new question has fewer than two terms of its own. Unconditional blending is how assistants end up answering the question you asked a minute ago.

A bigger context window would not have done this. A week of conversation does not fit in any window, and a rolling summary throws away the detail long before anyone asks for it. Memory is a retrieval problem, and the app already had a retrieval layer.

Measuring instead of reading three answers on a phone

For the first weeks every change to the prompt was judged by installing the app and reading a handful of answers. That is how the marker bug survived a week. The fix was a bench.

The phone journals every turn: the question, the exact fact sheet, the answer, and which model was loaded. That journal replays. One tool runs the camp's real questions and recorded fact sheets through the real model under different prompts. Another scores each answer with no model in the loop: does it contain a name, number or place that is not in the fact sheet? How much of it is lifted verbatim? How long is it? A third puts both models side by side on a local web page for a person to read.

Forty real questions, same fact sheets, two prompts. With the full rules: 2% of answers invent a camp particular, 20% are more than half copied from the sheet, median length 15 words. With no rules: 82% invent, 2% copy, median 56 words.

That table is the central trade-off of the project. The grounding rule buys truth and pays for it in voice. The flat, list-like answers people complain about are the price of the 2%. You cannot get the voice back by deleting rules, because deleting rules deletes the grounding. The bench is also why a fine-tune of the model on the camp's own voice did not ship: it learned the register and confabulated more, and the number said no.

The same bench settled a question that had been open for weeks. Answers had seemed to improve after the marker fix, but the improvement had come from switching to the smaller model at the same time. The small model reads freer, 51 words against 15, and invents a camp particular in 28% of answers. Part of what had felt like improvement was the grounding letting go.

There is now a regression gate on top: thirty-two questions with expected behaviour written down, run through a Python mirror of the phone's retrieval code before any change to it ships. The mirror is kept honest by tests that read the Swift source and refuse to pass if the stop list or the prompt has drifted.

Where it went wrong

Every one of these passed its tests. The pattern is that the likely failure in this system is silence, not a crash, so the work is making silence loud.

The meaning search shipped, installed, and did nothing
On the GPU, the encoder returned a vector of the right length full of NaN. NaN clears no threshold, so semantic recall added zero rows to every answer, with no error and no log. Every unit test had passed, because none of them ran the encoder: that needs a 333 MB model that is not in the test bundle. It runs on the CPU now, a live test loads the real model when it is present, and a non-finite vector is refused outright. A test that skips is not a test that passes.
Then it worked, and made answers worse
Once running, it fired on 13 of 15 real questions and added up to five rows each, about half of them noise. A question about one camper's tastes gained his battery specifications. The threshold had been set from one good question. Printed out, the similarities split cleanly: above 0.45 answered the question, below merely mentioned something in it. And 30% of the camp's facts are formulaic roster lines, "X signed up in 2019", which cluster so tightly that any question with a name or a year lands among them, scoring higher than genuine hits. The floor moved to 0.45 and the cap to two rows. A threshold calibrated on one example is a guess wearing a number.
A missing table returns nothing, not less
In SQLite, a query naming a table that does not exist fails to prepare, and the app saw an empty result rather than an error. One new table name in the person query killed every person card on older databases. Every query that names an optional table checks for it first now.
An index can lie
A partial index stores the result of its predicate at write time. Change the function behind it and queries keep using the old truth until the index is rebuilt. Two migrations exist for exactly this.
Recency rewarded missing data
Scoring undated facts as if they were current gave 160 facts with no year a threefold advantage. It surfaced as a failing test on a question nobody had predicted, which is the only way it was going to surface.
The roster was missing from every build for seven months
The script that installs the roster into the database needs a flag. Without it, it writes a file nothing reads and prints one line. Nobody read the line. A gate now refuses any build in which a table shrank more than 25% against the last build a human accepted, because the only reason this was caught at all was somebody watching a number scroll past.

Privacy by ownership, not by shape

The corpus is a decade of a real community's chat, and a slice of it ships on forty phones. What may ship is decided by asking whose a fact is, not what it looks like. A campmate's home address must never leave the Mac. The camp's own warehouse address absolutely must ship, because "where is the warehouse" is a real question. A shape rule, strip everything that looks like an address, gets exactly one of those right. What separates them in practice is context: a shipping-label block is personal; a sentence in a manual is the camp's.

The check runs on the artifact, not the code. A second, deliberately independent scanner reads the final database file before every release and refuses the build on a hit. It has disagreed with the builder once, catching ten personal phone numbers that a "safe" refactor exposed through a table the builder's own check had not scanned. That disagreement is the design working.

This page follows the same rule. Every screenshot shows the app's own chrome or the city's published listings. The people directory is blurred. The camp and its people stay in the dust.

The sync server, for when the desert ends

Campers capture notes and photos all week. A small FastAPI and Postgres service receives them whenever a phone finds coverage, and hands everyone else's back. It is built for connections that die halfway through, because that is the only kind there is on the drive out.

  • The note's id is minted on the phone. If the upload dies after the server saved it but before the phone heard back, the retry carries the same id and the server recognises it. Nothing is duplicated, because the server never assigns identity.
  • Feed order is a server sequence assigned under a lock. A phone catching up asks for everything since number N. Without the lock, a note whose transaction committed late could get a number the phone had already passed, and that phone would never see it.
  • Photos are stored by their content, and never deleted. Two phones photographing the same sign produce identical bytes and therefore one file. Deleting one member's note must not blind the other's, so deletion marks the note and leaves the file.

The round trip was verified on a real phone: a note with a photo and a voice memo went up, came back, and matched byte for byte with its EXIF intact.

Shipping

One developer, forty testers, and no staging environment in the desert. The release path compensates:

  • 199 unit tests, plus a harness that renders any screen of the app headlessly and saves the image. Every screenshot on this page came out of it, and layout bugs get caught by looking rather than hoping.
  • Resources are checked inside the built app, because the project generator drops a misplaced bundle entry silently. The tests open the artifact, not the source tree.
  • Debug and release are different apps with different bundle ids, so a tester's 3 GB model download is never invalidated by a debug build. The split is proven by running strings on the release binary, not trusted to compiler flags.
  • Unattended TestFlight uploads through the App Store Connect API. The build number is the commit count, so every build on a tester's phone names its exact commit.

What I would tell someone building one

  • Decide what the model is allowed to know, and write it down as a rule with a number attached. Here it is "camp particulars need a row" and the number is 2%. Every argument about voice, length and prompt wording is settled against that number.
  • Do the thinking at build time. A phone cannot understand ten years of chat, but it can search a catalogue that a much larger model prepared. Enrichment is where the intelligence is; retrieval and phrasing are what runs live.
  • Every claim carries its evidence. Not as a nice-to-have but as a constraint the database refuses to violate. It is the difference between an answer and a rumour.
  • Word search and meaning search fail differently, so use both and cap the second. Words are precise and miss synonyms; vectors reach synonyms and drift toward whatever is formulaic. Two rows from the vector side was the right number here, and it was found by printing the similarities, not by reasoning.
  • Memory is retrieval, not context. Write every turn to a database and search it. Label it as conversation, never as fact.
  • Assume the failure will be silent. Missing tables, NaN vectors, wrong chat markers and dropped resources all produced fluent, wrong output with no error. Floors, baselines, artifact scans and a journal are what turn silence into a red line.
  • Replay real questions. Reading three answers on a phone settled nothing for weeks. Forty questions through a bench settled the biggest design question in an afternoon, and overruled the owner's taste while doing it.
